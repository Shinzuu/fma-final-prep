1. Cost behaviour and the high-low method ★★★ Ch 22

Learn

  • Variable cost: the total changes in proportion to activity; the cost per unit stays the same (direct materials, direct labor).
  • Fixed cost: the total stays the same at every activity level in the relevant range; the cost per unit falls as activity rises (rent, depreciation).
  • Mixed cost: has a fixed part and a variable part (maintenance, utilities). Split it before any CVP work.
  • Relevant range: the activity range where the company expects to operate. The straight-line behaviour holds only inside it.
  • High-low method: use only the highest and lowest ACTIVITY levels (not the highest and lowest cost).
  • Variable cost per unit = (cost at high − cost at low) ÷ (high activity − low activity).
  • Fixed cost = total cost − (variable cost per unit × activity), at either the high or the low point. Both give the same answer; use the second as a check.
  • Estimated total cost = fixed cost + variable cost per unit × activity.
  • Quick test (Helena, S20): when activity doubles, a cost that doubles is variable, one that stays put is fixed, one that rises by less than double is mixed.

Worked example. Byrnes Company (S29, V.V.I). Units produced and total mixed cost: March 9,800 / $14,740; April 8,500 / $13,250; May 7,000 / $11,100; June 7,600 / $12,000; July 8,100 / $12,460. (a) Variable and fixed elements by high-low. (b) Total cost at 8,000 units.

  1. High activity = March 9,800 units, $14,740. Low activity = May 7,000 units, $11,100.
  2. Variable cost per unit = (14,740 − 11,100) ÷ (9,800 − 7,000) = 3,640 ÷ 2,800 = $1.30.
  3. Fixed = 14,740 − 1.30 × 9,800 (12,740) = $2,000. Check at low: 11,100 − 9,100 = $2,000.
  4. (b) 2,000 + 1.30 × 8,000 (10,400) = $12,400.

2. CVP income statement and contribution margin ★★ Ch 22

Learn

  • CVP analysis studies how costs, volume, selling price and product mix affect profit. Assumptions: costs split into fixed and variable, behaviour is linear in the relevant range, units produced = units sold, sales mix constant.
  • CVP income statement (internal use): Sales − Variable costs = Contribution margin; − Fixed costs = Net income. Show totals and per-unit columns.
  • Unit contribution margin = unit selling price − unit variable costs. Each unit sold adds this much to cover fixed costs, then to profit.
  • CM ratio = unit CM ÷ unit selling price (or total CM ÷ total sales). It is the CM in each sales dollar.
  • Above break-even, every extra unit raises net income by the unit CM.

3. Break-even, target net income, margin of safety ★★ Ch 22

Learn

  • Break-even point: total sales = total costs, so net income is zero. Three ways: equation (sales = variable costs + fixed costs), CM per unit, CM ratio.
  • Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ unit CM. Break-even sales dollars = fixed costs ÷ CM ratio.
  • Required sales for a target net income = (fixed costs + target net income) ÷ unit CM (units) or ÷ CM ratio (dollars).
  • Margin of safety = actual (or expected) sales − break-even sales. Margin of safety ratio = margin of safety ÷ actual sales. Higher means less risk of a loss.
  • CVP graph: the sales line starts at zero; the total-cost line starts at fixed costs; they cross at break-even.

Worked example. Zootsuit Inc. (S69, V.V.I): bags sell for $56; fixed costs $320,000; variable costs $42 a unit. (a) Break-even in dollars using the CM ratio. (b) Margin of safety and ratio if actual sales are $1,382,400. (c) Sales needed for net income of $410,000.

  1. CM ratio = (56 − 42) ÷ 56 = 14 ÷ 56 = 25%.
  2. (a) 320,000 ÷ 0.25 = $1,280,000.
  3. (b) 1,382,400 − 1,280,000 = $102,400; ratio 102,400 ÷ 1,382,400 = 7.4%.
  4. (c) (320,000 + 410,000) ÷ 0.25 = $2,920,000.

4. CVP with changes (Krisanne, Vargo cases) ★ Ch 22

Learn

  • Change one thing at a time: recompute unit CM (and CM ratio) with the new price or cost, then redo break-even, margin of safety or required sales.
  • A price cut lowers unit CM, so break-even rises and margin of safety falls. Compare total CM (units × unit CM) before and after to decide.
  • Cost changes: a change in variable cost changes unit CM; a change in fixed costs changes only the numerator.
  • Round break-even units UP to a whole unit.

Worked example. Krisanne (S82): 5,000 units at $60; variable $36 a unit; fixed $100,000; net income $20,000. Plan: cut price 10%, unit sales rise 25%. Break-even (units, dollars) and margin of safety (a) now, (b) with the plan; (c) comment.

  1. (a) CM = 60 − 36 = $24, ratio 40%. Break-even 100,000 ÷ 24 = 4,167 units; 100,000 ÷ 0.40 = $250,000. MoS 300,000 − 250,000 = $50,000.
  2. (b) New price $54, CM $18, ratio 18 ÷ 54. Break-even 100,000 ÷ 18 = 5,556 units; $300,000. Sales 6,250 × 54 = $337,500; MoS $37,500.
  3. (c) Total CM 6,250 × 18 = $112,500, which is $7,500 less than now. Break-even rises, margin of safety falls: do not implement.

7. Depreciation: straight-line, units-of-activity, declining-balance ★★★ Ch 10

Learn

  • Depreciation allocates the cost of a plant asset to expense over its useful life in a rational and systematic way. It is cost allocation, not valuation. Land is not depreciated.
  • Three factors: cost, useful life, salvage value. Depreciable cost = cost − salvage value. Book value = cost − accumulated depreciation.
  • Management selects the method it believes best measures an asset's contribution to revenue over its useful life (S17, the photo shared in class). Use in major U.S. companies: straight-line 83%, units-of-activity 5%, declining-balance 4%, other 8%.
  • Straight-line: depreciable cost ÷ useful life = the same expense each year.
  • Units-of-activity: depreciable cost ÷ total units = cost per unit; × units used in the year.
  • Declining-balance: book value at the start of the year × (2 × straight-line rate). Salvage is NOT subtracted first, but book value may not go below salvage, so the last year is cut.
  • Partial year: multiply by months owned ÷ 12. Bought April 1 → 9/12 in the first year and 3/12 in a sixth year.
  • Revised estimate: no entry for past years. New expense = (book value − new salvage) ÷ remaining life.
  • Entry each year: Depreciation Expense (income statement) / Accumulated Depreciation (balance sheet, contra asset).

9. FIFO, LIFO, average-cost (periodic) ★★★ Ch 6

Learn

  • Cost of goods available for sale = beginning inventory + purchases. It is split between ending inventory (balance sheet) and cost of goods sold (income statement).
  • Periodic: count ending units at year end, cost them, then COGS = goods available − ending inventory.
  • FIFO: the oldest costs are sold first, so ending inventory = the NEWEST costs.
  • LIFO: the newest costs are sold first, so ending inventory = the OLDEST costs.
  • Average-cost: weighted-average unit cost = cost of goods available ÷ units available; ending inventory = ending units × that cost.
  • When prices rise: FIFO gives the highest ending inventory and net income; LIFO gives the highest COGS and lowest income tax; average is in between.
  • Specific identification: track the actual cost of each item sold (Crivitz: COGS 700 + 800 = $1,500).

10. Perpetual vs periodic ★★★ Ch 6

Learn

  • Perpetual: detailed records of each purchase and sale; inventory on hand is known at all times; COGS is recorded at each sale. Better control.
  • Periodic: no detailed records; COGS is found only at period end by a physical count (beginning + purchases − ending).
  • Perpetual FIFO gives the SAME result as periodic FIFO. Perpetual LIFO and moving-average usually differ, because the cost is fixed at each sale date from the goods on hand THEN.
  • Moving-average: compute a new average after every purchase; each sale uses the current average.

13. Closing entries and the accounting cycle ★★★ Ch 4

Learn

  • Temporary accounts (closed): revenues, expenses, owner's drawings. Permanent accounts (not closed): assets, liabilities, owner's capital.
  • Closing entries move net income (or loss) and drawings into owner's capital and leave each temporary account at zero.
  • Four entries: (1) revenues → Income Summary; (2) expenses → Income Summary; (3) Income Summary → Owner's Capital; (4) Drawings → Owner's Capital (never to Income Summary).
  • Post-closing trial balance: only permanent accounts; proves they balance before the next period.
  • Accounting cycle: 1 analyze transactions; 2 journalize; 3 post to ledger; 4 trial balance; 5 journalize and post adjusting entries; 6 adjusted trial balance; 7 financial statements; 8 journalize and post closing entries; 9 post-closing trial balance.
  • Correcting entries: made when an error is found, posted before closing. Correcting entry = correct entry − wrong entry.

Worked example. Pioneer Advertising (S20–S22): Service Revenue 10,600; expenses total 7,740; Drawings 500; Owner's Capital 10,000. Closing entries and capital after closing.

  2. Income Summary 7,740 / each expense.
  3. Income Summary 2,860 / Owner's Capital 2,860.
  4. Owner's Capital 500 / Owner's Drawings 500.
  5. Capital 10,000 + 2,860 − 500 = $12,360; post-closing trial balance totals $21,950.

Learn

  • Assets: current assets; long-term investments; property, plant, and equipment; intangible assets. Then current liabilities; long-term liabilities; owner's equity.
  • Current assets: expected to become cash or be used up within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is LONGER. Listed in order of liquidity: cash, short-term investments, receivables, inventory, supplies, prepaid items.

16. Multiple-step income statement; COGS under periodic ★★★ Ch 5

Learn

  • Multiple-step: several steps to net income; separates operating from non-operating activities.
  • Net sales = sales revenue − sales returns and allowances − sales discounts. Gross profit = net sales − COGS. Income from operations = gross profit − operating expenses. Net income = income from operations + other revenues and gains − other expenses and losses.

  • There is NO investing activities section on an income statement.
  • COGS (periodic) = beginning inventory + cost of goods purchased − ending inventory, where cost of goods purchased = purchases − returns − discounts + freight-in.
