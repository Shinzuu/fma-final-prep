1 Cost behaviour and the high-low method

Theory

  • A variable cost changes in total in direct proportion to activity, while the cost per unit stays the same. Examples: direct materials and direct labor.
  • A fixed cost stays the same in total at every activity level within the relevant range, so the cost per unit falls as activity rises. Examples: rent and depreciation.
  • A mixed cost has both a fixed part and a variable part, such as maintenance or utilities. Split it into the two parts before any CVP work.
  • The relevant range is the range of activity over which the company expects to operate. Costs behave in a straight line only inside it.
  • The high-low method splits a mixed cost using only two points: the highest and the lowest activity level. Pick the points by activity, never by cost.
  • Work out the fixed cost at the high point, then repeat at the low point. Both must give the same answer, so the second calculation checks the first.
  • Quick test from Helena Company (S20): when activity doubles, a cost that doubles is variable, a cost that does not change is fixed, and a cost that rises by less than double is mixed.

Variable cost per unit = (cost at high − cost at low) ÷ (high activity − low activity)

Fixed cost = total cost − (variable cost per unit × activity)

Total cost = fixed cost + (variable cost per unit × activity)

Traps

  • Pick the high and low points by activity, not by cost (Metro: April, not March).
  • Check the fixed cost at both points; if they differ, the variable rate is wrong.

Example from the slides

Worked example

Byrnes Company (S29, V.V.I). Units produced and total mixed cost: March 9,800 / $14,740; April 8,500 / $13,250; May 7,000 / $11,100; June 7,600 / $12,000; July 8,100 / $12,460. (a) Variable and fixed elements by high-low. (b) Total cost at 8,000 units.

  1. High activity = March 9,800 units, $14,740. Low activity = May 7,000 units, $11,100.
  2. Variable cost per unit = (14,740 − 11,100) ÷ (9,800 − 7,000) = 3,640 ÷ 2,800 = $1.30.
  3. Fixed = 14,740 − 1.30 × 9,800 (12,740) = $2,000. Check at low: 11,100 − 9,100 = $2,000.
  4. (b) 2,000 + 1.30 × 8,000 (10,400) = $12,400.

2 CVP income statement and contribution margin

Theory

  • Cost-volume-profit (CVP) analysis studies how selling price, costs, volume and sales mix affect profit.
  • It assumes that costs can be split into fixed and variable parts, that behaviour is linear within the relevant range, that units produced equal units sold, and that the sales mix stays constant.
  • A CVP income statement is for internal use. It subtracts variable costs from sales to show the contribution margin, then subtracts fixed costs to reach net income. Show both a total column and a per-unit column.
  • The unit contribution margin is what each unit sold adds toward covering fixed costs, and then toward profit.
  • The contribution margin ratio is the share of each sales dollar that is contribution margin.
  • Once fixed costs are covered, every extra unit sold raises net income by exactly the unit contribution margin.

Unit contribution margin = unit selling price − unit variable cost

Contribution margin ratio = unit contribution margin ÷ unit selling price

Net income = contribution margin − fixed costs

Traps

  • Unit CM uses unit variable cost only; fixed costs never enter it.
  • The CM ratio is CM ÷ sales, not net income ÷ sales.

Example from the slides

Worked example

Ampco Industries (S46, V.V.I): unit price $85, unit variable costs $32, monthly fixed costs $190,000, 4,000 units sold in September. Prepare the CVP income statement with per-unit and total values.

  1. Sales 4,000 × 85 = $340,000; variable costs 4,000 × 32 = $128,000.
  2. Contribution margin 340,000 − 128,000 = $212,000 ($53 a unit).
  3. Net income 212,000 − 190,000 = $22,000 (table above). CM ratio 53 ÷ 85 = 62.35%.

3 Break-even, target net income, margin of safety

Theory

  • At the break-even point, total revenues equal total costs (fixed and variable), so net income is zero. Slide S48 gives three approaches: the mathematical equation, the contribution margin technique (unit contribution margin for units, contribution margin ratio for dollars), and the CVP graph.
  • Break-even can be expressed in sales units or in sales dollars.
  • To find the sales needed for a target net income, add the target to fixed costs before dividing.
  • The margin of safety is how far actual (or expected) sales can fall before the company makes a loss. A higher margin of safety means less risk.
  • On the CVP graph, the sales line starts at zero and the total-cost line starts at the level of fixed costs. The two lines cross at the break-even point.

Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ unit contribution margin

Break-even sales dollars = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin ratio

Required sales = (fixed costs + target net income) ÷ unit CM (units) or ÷ CM ratio (dollars)

Margin of safety = actual sales − break-even sales; ratio = margin of safety ÷ actual sales

Traps

  • Units: divide by unit CM. Dollars: divide by the CM ratio.
  • Margin of safety ratio = MoS ÷ actual sales, not ÷ break-even sales.
  • Target net income is added to fixed costs before dividing.

Example from the slides

Worked example

Zootsuit Inc. (S69, V.V.I): bags sell for $56; fixed costs $320,000; variable costs $42 a unit. (a) Break-even in dollars using the CM ratio. (b) Margin of safety and ratio if actual sales are $1,382,400. (c) Sales needed for net income of $410,000.

  1. CM ratio = (56 − 42) ÷ 56 = 14 ÷ 56 = 25%.
  2. (a) 320,000 ÷ 0.25 = $1,280,000.
  3. (b) 1,382,400 − 1,280,000 = $102,400; ratio 102,400 ÷ 1,382,400 = 7.4%.
  4. (c) (320,000 + 410,000) ÷ 0.25 = $2,920,000.

4 CVP with changes (Krisanne, Vargo cases)

Theory

  • Change one factor at a time. Recalculate the unit contribution margin (and the ratio) with the new price or cost, then redo break-even, margin of safety or required sales.
  • A price cut lowers the unit contribution margin, so break-even rises and the margin of safety falls. To decide, compare total contribution margin (units × unit contribution margin) before and after the change.
  • A change in variable cost changes the unit contribution margin. A change in fixed costs changes only the amount being divided.
  • Show the exact break-even figure first (200,000 ÷ 150 = 1,333.33). The slide prints 1,333 (rounded); to actually reach break-even the company must sell 1,334.

New break-even units = fixed costs ÷ new unit contribution margin

Required units = (fixed costs + target net income) ÷ unit contribution margin

Traps

  • Recompute the unit CM with the new price or cost before anything else.
  • Judge a price cut by total CM (units × CM), not by sales revenue.

Example from the slides

Worked example

Krisanne (S82): 5,000 units at $60; variable $36 a unit; fixed $100,000; net income $20,000. Plan: cut price 10%, unit sales rise 25%. Break-even (units, dollars) and margin of safety (a) now, (b) with the plan; (c) comment.

  1. (a) CM = 60 − 36 = $24, ratio 40%. Break-even 100,000 ÷ 24 = 4,167 units; 100,000 ÷ 0.40 = $250,000. MoS 300,000 − 250,000 = $50,000.
  2. (b) New price $54, CM $18, ratio 18 ÷ 54. Break-even 100,000 ÷ 18 = 5,556 units; $300,000. Sales 6,250 × 54 = $337,500; MoS $37,500.
  3. (c) Total CM 6,250 × 18 = $112,500, which is $7,500 less than now. Break-even rises, margin of safety falls: do not implement.

5 Absorption vs variable costing (appendix)

Theory

  • Under absorption costing, product cost includes all manufacturing costs, including a share of fixed manufacturing overhead.
  • Under variable costing, product cost includes only variable manufacturing costs. All fixed manufacturing overhead is expensed in the period.
  • When more units are produced than sold, absorption costing reports higher net income, because part of the fixed overhead stays in ending inventory. When production equals sales, both methods give the same net income.

Absorption unit cost = variable manufacturing cost + (fixed overhead ÷ units produced)

Variable unit cost = variable manufacturing cost only

Traps

  • Absorption puts fixed overhead into unit cost; variable costing expenses all of it.
  • Produced = sold gives the same net income under both.

6 Cost of plant assets

Theory

  • Plant assets have physical substance, are used in the operations of the business, are not held for sale to customers, and are expected to be useful for a number of years.
  • Under the historical cost principle, a plant asset is recorded at cost: every expenditure needed to acquire it and make it ready for its intended use.
  • Land cost includes the price, closing costs (attorney's and title fees), the broker's commission, liens assumed, and the net cost of removing an old building. Land is not depreciated.
  • Land improvements (driveways, parking lots, fences, landscaping, sprinklers) have limited lives, so they are recorded separately from land and depreciated (S8).
  • Buildings: a purchased building's cost includes the price, closing costs and broker's commission, plus remodeling and repairs to get it ready; a constructed building's cost includes the contract price, architect's fees, building permits and excavation (S9).
  • Equipment cost includes the price, sales taxes, freight, insurance while in transit, and the cost of assembly, installation and testing. A motor vehicle license or an insurance policy bought after delivery is an operating cost, not part of the asset.
  • Ordinary repairs are expensed to Maintenance and Repair Expense (revenue expenditures). Additions and improvements that increase efficiency, capacity or useful life are debited to the asset (capital expenditures).

Cost of plant asset = all expenditures to acquire it and make it ready for use

Traps

  • Net removal cost of an old building goes to Land (costs less salvage).
  • Land is not depreciated; land improvements are.
  • License and insurance after delivery are expensed, not added to the asset.

7 Depreciation: straight-line, units-of-activity, declining-balance

Theory

  • Depreciation allocates the cost of a plant asset to expense over its useful life in a rational and systematic way. It is a process of cost allocation, not of valuation. Land is not depreciated.
  • Three factors drive depreciation: cost, useful life and salvage value.
  • Management selects the method it believes best measures the asset's contribution to revenue over its useful life (S17, the slide photo shared in class). In major U.S. companies, 83% use straight-line, 5% units-of-activity, 4% declining-balance and 8% other methods.
  • Straight-line gives the same expense every year. Units-of-activity follows actual use. Declining-balance is an accelerated method: the expense is highest in the first year and falls each year after.
  • Declining-balance does not subtract salvage value first, but book value may never fall below salvage value, so the final year's expense is reduced to stop there.
  • For a partial year, multiply by the months owned ÷ 12. An asset bought on April 1 gets 9/12 of a year at first, and the remaining 3/12 in a sixth year.
  • A revised estimate needs no entry for past years. Spread the remaining depreciable amount over the remaining life.
  • For income tax, the IRS does not require the same method as the financial statements. Taxpayers use straight-line or MACRS, an accelerated method; MACRS is not acceptable under GAAP (S30).
  • Each year's entry: debit Depreciation Expense (income statement), credit Accumulated Depreciation (balance sheet, a contra asset).

Depreciable cost = cost − salvage value; book value = cost − accumulated depreciation

Straight-line = depreciable cost ÷ useful life

Units-of-activity = (depreciable cost ÷ total units) × units used this year

Declining-balance = book value at start of year × (2 × straight-line rate)

Revised depreciation = (book value − new salvage value) ÷ remaining useful life

8 Disposal of plant assets: retirement and sale

Theory

  • A company disposes of a plant asset in one of three ways: retirement, sale or exchange.
  • First, record depreciation up to the date of disposal.
  • Then remove the asset: debit Accumulated Depreciation for its full balance and credit the asset account for its original cost.
  • If the proceeds exceed book value, record a gain; if they are less, record a loss. A loss on disposal is reported under other expenses and losses.
  • In a retirement no cash is received. A fully depreciated asset gives no gain or loss (Hobart). Any book value left becomes a loss (Sunset: 18,000 − 14,000 = $4,000).

Book value = cost − accumulated depreciation (updated to the disposal date)

Gain or loss = proceeds − book value

Traps

  • Record depreciation to the disposal date first.
  • Credit the asset at original cost; gain or loss = proceeds − book value.

9 FIFO, LIFO and average-cost (periodic)

Theory

  • Cost of goods available for sale is split between ending inventory (balance sheet) and cost of goods sold (income statement).
  • Under the periodic system, count the units on hand at year end, cost them with the chosen method, then subtract to find cost of goods sold.
  • FIFO (first-in, first-out) assumes the oldest costs are sold first, so ending inventory carries the newest costs.
  • LIFO (last-in, first-out) assumes the newest costs are sold first, so ending inventory carries the oldest costs.
  • Average-cost applies one weighted-average unit cost to both ending inventory and cost of goods sold.
  • When prices are rising, FIFO reports the highest ending inventory and net income; LIFO reports the highest cost of goods sold and the lowest income tax; average-cost falls in between.
  • Specific identification tracks the actual cost of each item sold (Crivitz: cost of goods sold 700 + 800 = $1,500).

Goods available = beginning inventory + purchases

Cost of goods sold = goods available − ending inventory

Weighted-average unit cost = cost of goods available ÷ units available

10 Perpetual vs periodic

Theory

  • Under the perpetual system, the company keeps detailed records of every purchase and sale, so inventory on hand is known at all times and cost of goods sold is recorded with each sale. It gives better control.
  • Under the periodic system, there are no detailed records. Cost of goods sold is found only at the end of the period, from a physical count.
  • Perpetual FIFO gives the same result as periodic FIFO. Perpetual LIFO and moving-average usually differ, because the cost of each sale is taken from the goods on hand on the sale date.
  • Moving-average recomputes the average unit cost after every purchase, and each sale uses the average at that moment.

Moving-average unit cost = cost of goods on hand ÷ units on hand (after each purchase)

13 Closing entries and the accounting cycle

Theory

  • Temporary accounts are closed at year end: revenues, expenses and owner's drawings. Permanent accounts are carried forward: assets, liabilities and owner's capital.
  • Closing entries transfer net income (or net loss) and drawings to owner's capital and leave every temporary account with a zero balance.
  • Owner's drawings are closed directly to Owner's Capital, never to Income Summary.
  • The post-closing trial balance lists only permanent accounts and proves that they balance before the next period begins.
  • Correcting entries are made as soon as an error is found and must be posted before closing. The correcting entry is the difference between the correct entry and the wrong one.

Closing: (1) revenues → Income Summary; (2) expenses → Income Summary; (3) Income Summary → Owner's Capital; (4) Drawings → Owner's Capital

Accounting cycle: analyze → journalize → post → trial balance → adjusting entries → adjusted trial balance → financial statements → closing entries → post-closing trial balance

Traps

  • Drawings are closed to Owner's Capital, never to Income Summary.

14 Classified balance sheet

Theory

  • A classified balance sheet groups assets into current assets; long-term investments; property, plant, and equipment; and intangible assets. Liabilities are split into current and long-term, followed by owner's equity.
  • Current assets are expected to become cash or be used up within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer. They are listed in order of liquidity: cash, short-term investments, receivables, inventory, supplies, prepaid items.
  • Long-term investments include stocks and bonds of other companies, land or buildings not used in operations, and long-term notes receivable.
  • Property, plant, and equipment are long-lived assets used in operations, shown at cost less accumulated depreciation. Intangible assets have no physical substance: patents, goodwill, trademarks.
  • Current liabilities are due within one year (accounts payable, salaries payable, unearned revenue, current notes). Long-term liabilities are due after one year (for example, a mortgage).
  • Owner's equity: a proprietorship has one capital account, a partnership one per partner, and a corporation shows common stock and retained earnings.

Total assets = total liabilities + owner's equity

Net sales → gross profit → operating expenses → income from operations → other revenues/gains and other expenses/losses → net income. [S41–S46]

S4Give the two reasons for using the single-step format.

Show answer
No profit is realized until total revenues exceed total expenses; the format is simpler and easier to read. [S48]

S5Distinguish multiple-step and single-step income statements.

Show answer
Multiple-step: several steps, separates operating and non-operating. Single-step: total revenues − total expenses. Same net income. [S40, S48]
